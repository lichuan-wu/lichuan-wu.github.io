Ocean surface waves sit at the interface between the atmosphere and the ocean, and they control much of what passes across it. Wind-generated waves take up momentum from the atmosphere and release it to the ocean when they break; swell waves that run faster than the wind can even push momentum back upward into the marine atmospheric boundary layer. Most weather and climate models still treat the sea surface as if these processes did not exist.

Our work combines tower and buoy observations (for example at the Östergarnsholm station in the Baltic Sea), turbulence theory and coupled atmosphere–wave–ocean modelling to:

- quantify how waves redistribute the air–sea momentum and turbulent kinetic energy fluxes;
- describe the wind profile and turbulence in the wave boundary layer, including under swell;
- parameterise wave-induced upper-ocean mixing, wave breaking and sea spray;
- estimate how waves change the global air–sea CO₂ flux.

A new Crafoord-funded stereo video system at Östergarnsholm (2026–2027) will measure the sea surface directly and connect wave and surface heterogeneity to the turbulent fluxes.
